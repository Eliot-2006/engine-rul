Remaining useful life of turbofan engines

EngineRUL predicts how many operating cycles a jet engine has left before failure, from the history of its 21 sensors. It compares two naive baselines, Ridge regression, gradient boosting and a PyTorch LSTM on NASA's simulated C-MAPSS FD001 benchmark under one leakage-safe protocol, then stress-tests the result with nested cross-validation, ablations and conformal prediction intervals.

Why gradient boosting beat the LSTM here

  • Little data. 80 training engines give about 14,000 heavily overlapping 30-cycle windows: enough for a shallow tree ensemble, thin for a recurrent network.
  • Engineered trend features. Gradient boosting sees each sensor's last value, mean, spread and slope over the 30-cycle window. In the ablations, dropping the slopes costs it about 5 RMSE while the spread adds nothing measurable: the degradation trend carries much of the signal, and the tree model gets it as an explicit input.
  • Noisy selection. On the 20 Phase-1 validation engines the LSTM ranked first; on the test set and in 15-fold nested CV gradient boosting did. With so few engines, single-split rankings are fragile.

These are explanations consistent with the evidence, not controlled experiments; a larger dataset (FD002–FD004) could change the ranking.

Why engine-grouped and nested? Windows from the same engine are highly correlated, so every engine's data stays on one side of each split; otherwise a model is scored on engines it has partly seen. Hyperparameters are chosen in inner folds only, so the outer-fold score is not inflated by the selection itself.

Limitations and caveats

  • Simulated data, one subset. C-MAPSS FD001 has one operating condition and one fault mode, and the test set has only 100 engines, so confidence intervals are wide. Nothing here is validated for real aircraft.
  • Different protocols. Official test numbers use one cut point per engine and uncapped labels; validation numbers use many cut points and truth capped at 125. They are never compared directly.
  • Approximate CIs. Repeated CV folds share training engines, so engine bootstrap intervals on pooled CV predictions likely understate training-set variability; fold-to-fold spread is shown alongside.
  • Seeds. Gradient boosting's 5 seeds give bit-identical models, so they are not independent evidence of stability. Ridge and the baselines are deterministic and trained once. LSTM ablations use one CV repeat.
  • Interval assumptions. Conformal coverage is marginal, assumes exchangeable engines and cut points drawn like the calibration ones, and does not carry over to the official test set's truncation process.
